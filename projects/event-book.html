How good are my calls before earnings?

Before a company reports, I write down probabilities for what will happen and compare them with what the options market prices. After the release, I score every call, right or wrong. The point is the process: if I am wrong, the reasoning should still have been sound.

How it works

Before the release I publish four things: the move the options price for the day, how the stock moved on past results days, the evidence for and against, and a probability for each question. Every page is committed to a public GitHub repository, so the time stamp shows it was written beforehand.

Reading the options. A straddle is a call and a put at the same strike, so it pays off if the stock moves far in either direction. Its price, as a share of the stock price, is roughly the move the market expects. When the first option expiry after the release also covers some normal days, I take out the variance of those normal days to isolate the results day. Then I compare that number with how the stock actually moved on past results days. If history moved more than the options price, the options look cheap, but only if the gap is wider than the bid-ask spread.

After the release each probability gets a Brier score: the squared gap between my probability and what happened (1 if it happened, 0 if not). Zero is perfect, and always saying 50% scores 0.25. Below 0.25 means the calls added something.

Burberry, half-year results, 12 November 2026

Burberry is two years into a turnaround under its CEO, Joshua Schulman. The results come out at 07:00 London time on Thursday 12 November and cover the six months to late September. The key new number is comparable store sales for July to September, the second quarter of its financial year. The shares traded at about 1,094p on 11 November (London Stock Exchange, ticker BRBY).

What the options price

A move of about 4.6% on the day.

What history says

6.9% on average, 5.3% median. Cheap at the mid price, but the spread eats the edge.

What the options price

Burberry options trade on ICE Futures Europe. The first expiry after the release is Friday 20 November, so the option covers the results day plus six normal trading days. Delayed quotes for the 1,100p strike on 11 November:

How I get from 6.0% to 4.6%. The straddle prices the whole period to 20 November, not just Thursday. Six normal days at Burberry's recent daily volatility (1.9%, from the last 30 days) account for part of the price. Taking their variance out of the total leaves an expected move of about 4.6% for the results day itself.

How much I trust it. Not much. The quotes are delayed, the spread is wide, and open interest at this strike is close to zero, so the range runs from 3.1% to 5.9% depending on where you trade. Implied volatility for the stock is about 34% a year against about 31% realised over the last 30 days, so the options charge only a small premium for the event.

12 of the 19 days moved more than the 4.6% priced. But 2024 and early 2025 were extreme: a profit warning, a CEO change and the new plan. The last four days averaged 5.2%, closer to the price.

Would I buy the straddle? At the mid price, it breaks even if the stock ends more than about 6% away from 1,100p by 20 November. Results days alone did that 8 times out of 19. At the ask, it needs about 7%, which happened 6 times out of 19. The options look cheap against the full history, but not by enough to pay the spread on quotes I cannot trade live. So no trade: the view goes into the last probability below instead.

What I saw in China. Burberry is very visible in Shanghai: stores across the city, and people like to be seen wearing it. Fakes are getting more common, which tells me the brand is wanted. Younger buyers go for the puffers and belts. Wealthier men and women buy the clothing, scarves, shoes and bags. Burberry itself credits Gen Z for its China growth, which matches what I saw.

Two limits on that view. Fakes show demand for the brand, not sales for Burberry; some of that demand never reaches a Burberry till. And most puffer sales come after September, so they matter more for the January update than for this quarter. What my view does support is that China growth is real demand, not a one-quarter bounce. That is why I put the China question at 50% even though the base is 7 points harder.

Result, scored 13 November

Burberry beat on sales and, by a wide margin, on profit. Second-quarter comparable store sales grew 4% (Americas +11%, Greater China +5%, Asia Pacific +3%, Europe and Middle East −2%). Half-year adjusted operating profit was £62m, against £19m a year earlier and about £44m expected; wholesale grew 17%. The shares closed at 1,180.5p, up 8.0% from 1,093.0p, against the 4.6% the options priced.

Verdict: slightly better than a coin flip (0.23 against 0.25), and for partly the wrong reasons.

What I got right. Group sales growth slowed from 5% to 4%, as the harder comparison base suggested. The options were too cheap: the stock moved 8.0% against 4.6% priced, in line with Burberry's history of large results-day moves. I did not buy the straddle because of the spread, and in hindsight it would have paid.

What I got wrong. I had no lean on profit and set the bar at one broker's estimate (UBS, £39m) when the market expected about £44m; the result, £62m, beat both by far, mostly through wholesale (+17%) and cost savings. On China, the base effect did not bite the way my two-year arithmetic said (about +2%); it came in at +5%, which supports the view from Shanghai that the demand is real. And I leaned against the shares rising because of the run-up and the "no acceleration" pattern; a profit beat of this size made that irrelevant. Lesson: a sales question needs a profit view next to it, and the bar should be the consensus, not the most convenient broker number.

Richemont, half-year results, 13 November 2026

Richemont owns Cartier and Van Cleef & Arpels. Jewellery is about three quarters of sales. The results come out at 07:00 CET on Friday 13 November. The shares traded at about CHF 175 on 11 November (SIX Swiss Exchange, ticker CFR).

What the options price

A move of about 6.3% on the day.

What history says

6.9% on average on November results days. Fairly priced, so no volatility trade.

What the options price

Richemont options trade on Eurex, and there is a weekly expiry on Friday 13 November, the results day itself. So the option covers only Thursday, a normal day, and Friday, the results. Delayed quotes for the at-the-money straddle (strikes CHF 174 and 176) on 11 November, with the shares at CHF 175.40:

13 November weekly straddleBidMidAsk
Straddle (call + put)CHF 8.8CHF 11.0CHF 13.3
As % of the share price5.0%6.3%7.6%

Why I use 6.3% almost as it is. Only one normal day sits inside the option. Taking out Thursday's variance, at Richemont's recent daily volatility of about 1.9%, leaves about 6.1% for Friday alone. The gap is small, so I kept 6.3% as the bar for the question below, which if anything is slightly generous to the market.

How much I trust it. More than Burberry's, because the expiry sits right on the event, but the quotes are still delayed and the spread is wide: depending on where you trade, the price says anything from 5.0% to 7.6%.

Unlike some companies, Richemont's half-year results are real news: second-quarter sales and the margins come out at the same time. A 6 to 7% move is normal, and the options price about that.

Would I buy or sell the straddle? Neither. At the mid price it pays off if the stock moves more than 6.3% by Friday's close, which happened on 7 of the 15 results days. Buying at the ask needs 7.6%, which happened 4 times. Selling at the bid loses if the move is above 5.0%, which happened 10 times. Whichever side I take, the spread costs more than the gap between the price and history, so the volatility view is neutral and the edge, if any, is in direction.

Update, 11 Nov evening: after the call was first published at 17:51 Shanghai time, I added the options and straddle detail, put the evidence into a table, and added the median and the last four results days to the history table, to match the other calls. The last four averaged only 3.6%, which argues for a smaller move than my 45% implies; I am leaving the probabilities as published.

What would prove me wrong

Second-quarter growth below 10%, a drop in Hong Kong and Macau after the strong summer data, or a gross margin that holds up despite gold, which would mean price increases did more than I assume.

Result, scored 14 November

Sales were in line, the margin beat, and the shares fell. Second-quarter sales grew 13% at constant exchange rates; jewellery grew 14%, below the 16% RBC's survey expected and down from 24% in the first quarter. China, Hong Kong and Macau grew only 7%, and the Middle East and Africa fell 7% as tourist spending in Dubai dropped. The half-year operating margin was 25.5% against 22.2% a year earlier, or 23.6% without €229m of one-off tariff gains. The shares closed at CHF 169.45, down 3.8% from CHF 176.15, a smaller move than the 6.3% priced.

Verdict: worse than a coin flip (0.33 against 0.25). Three of four calls leaned the wrong way.

Sales. Richemont reports growth in whole percentages, and 13% does not clear "more than 13%". Even if the unrounded figure was a fraction above, the call was wrong in substance: I expected a fifth straight beat and got a result in line, with jewellery below expectations.

What I missed. The Middle East. I looked hard at Hong Kong and China and not at all at the Gulf, where sales fell 7% after growing 22% a year earlier. And the Hong Kong evidence, including my own view from the queues, did not carry through: China, Hong Kong and Macau grew 7%, not the double digits I had in mind. Retail data for Hong Kong as a whole was a poor guide to one company's sales there.

Margins. I worried about gold costs and tariffs; the margin rose even without the one-off gains (23.6%). Volume growth and price increases did more than I gave them credit for.

What held up. The volatility view. I put the chance of a bigger-than-priced move below even, and the last four results days averaged only 3.6%; the stock moved 3.8%. Selling the straddle would have paid, though I said no trade because of the spread.

Lesson. Check every region that grew fast last year, not only the one I know best. A comparison base works region by region, and the Gulf had the hardest one.
